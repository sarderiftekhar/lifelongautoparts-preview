California compliance

California engine swaps and the smog referee

If your vehicle is registered in California, the engine you fit is not purely a mechanical decision. It is an emissions one, and getting it wrong means a vehicle you cannot register.

Last updated 18 August 2026

This is orientation, not legal advice

The rules are administered by the California Bureau of Automotive Repair and they change. Confirm anything you are about to spend money on with BAR or a licensed referee station directly.

Why a referee, and when

California requires most vehicles to pass a periodic smog check. A standard smog station tests emissions and inspects the equipment. It is not equipped to certify a vehicle whose engine is not the one it left the factory with.

That is what the referee programme is for. A referee station is a state-contracted facility with the authority to inspect a modified vehicle and certify it as compliant. If your engine has been changed to something that differs from the original, this is the route to a legal registration.

The engine family rule

Every engine certified for sale in the United States carries an engine family designation, sometimes called a test group. It appears on the emissions label under the bonnet, and it identifies the exact emissions configuration the engine was certified in.

This is the single most important detail in the whole process, and it is the one most often skipped. Two engines can be the same displacement, the same cylinder count and physically interchangeable while belonging to different engine families - because the calibration, the catalyst or the fuel system differs. From an emissions standpoint they are different engines.

It is also why we ask for a VIN before quoting an engine for a California vehicle. It is the only reliable way to establish which family the original engine belonged to.

What the referee actually checks

  • That the engine is legal for the vehicle - see the next section
  • That all the emissions equipment for that engine is present and connected
  • That the on-board diagnostics system functions and reports no relevant faults
  • That the vehicle passes a tailpipe test where applicable
  • That the emissions label is present and legible

The equipment check is where most failures happen. An engine fitted without its original catalyst, air injection or evaporative components will not certify, regardless of how cleanly it runs.

Which engine you are allowed to fit

The principles have been stable for a long time:

  • Same year or newer. You may fit an engine from the same model year as the vehicle, or a newer one. You may not go backwards.
  • Same vehicle class. An engine certified for a passenger car goes into a passenger car. Moving between classes generally does not certify.
  • All the emissions equipment comes too. Whatever that engine was certified with must be fitted and functioning.
  • Federal-only engines are a problem. An engine certified to a federal standard rather than a California one may not be acceptable.

What to establish before you buy

  1. Your original engine family. From the emissions label under the bonnet, or from the VIN.
  2. The engine family of the replacement. Ask the seller. If they cannot tell you, that is a reason to buy elsewhere.
  3. What emissions equipment is included. Get it in writing on the quote, not verbally.
  4. Whether your shop has done a California swap before. The installation matters as much as the part.

What we do about this

We capture the engine family from your VIN at the point of quoting, and we check it against the unit before anything ships. It is a fitment accuracy requirement for us, not an optional extra - a California customer with the wrong engine family has bought a vehicle they cannot register.

The mistakes that fail an inspection

  • Buying on model name alone, without checking the engine family
  • Fitting an older engine than the vehicle
  • Leaving off emissions equipment because the engine "runs fine without it"
  • Removing or losing the emissions label
  • Clearing diagnostic codes just before the inspection, so the readiness monitors have not completed
  • Assuming a federal-specification engine will pass

Common questions

Can I put any engine in a California car?

No. The replacement must be from the same year as the vehicle or newer, and it must be certified for the same class of vehicle. The emissions equipment that came with that engine has to come with it and has to be connected and working.

Do I need a referee for a like-for-like replacement?

If you replace the engine with an identical one - same engine family, same year, same configuration - it is generally not a change requiring referee certification. It is when the replacement differs that a referee gets involved. Confirm your specific case with BAR rather than relying on any website, including this one.

What does a referee inspection cost?

BAR sets the fee and it changes. Check the current figure on the BAR referee programme site before budgeting.

Can I fit a diesel engine into a car that had gasoline?

Not without going through the referee process, and in most cases the answer will be no. Engine swaps across fuel types very rarely certify.

What if my vehicle is registered outside California?

None of this applies in the same way. It becomes relevant if the vehicle is later registered in California.

Buying an engine for a California vehicle?

Send us the VIN. We will tell you the engine family before you commit to anything, and we will not quote you a unit that cannot certify.
